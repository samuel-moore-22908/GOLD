# What the panel shows

Draft prose for `figures/gold_panel.png`, written for an educated general
reader. Every figure quoted here is computed by `gold_panel.do` from the same
Census pull the chart is drawn from; the phase-average gold price used in the
final caveat comes from `data/processed/efp_dislocation_v2.csv` (LBMA PM).

---

Gold futures traded in New York are settled, if the buyer insists, in metal: a
seller who does not close out has to place approved bars in an exchange-licensed
vault inside the United States. For years that obligation cost almost nothing,
because bullion could be moved from London or Zurich to New York for freight and
a few days' interest, which is why the two prices tracked each other so closely.
The tariff threat of late 2024 broke the assumption. If imported gold were caught
by a duty, anyone carrying a short position in New York against metal held abroad
faced a bill of unknown size to honour it, and the market started paying for
metal that was already inside the customs border — New York futures opened a
premium over London spot far wider than financing costs could explain. The
sensible response was to move the metal in before any duty could land. London's
400-ounce bars are not deliverable against a New York contract, so most of the
flow was routed through Swiss refineries to be recast into the 100-ounce and kilo
bars the exchange accepts, which is why Switzerland dominates the right-hand
panel. Over the five months from November 2024 to March 2025, American gold
imports averaged $19.4bn a month against $2.0bn a month in the year before — a
tenfold rise in value, and roughly eightfold in weight once the higher gold price
is netted out. Gold, the twentieth-largest American import heading in the
baseline year, became the largest of all, ahead of cars and computers.

None of it was consumed. The metal sat in vaults a few miles from where the
futures traded, waiting for the question to be settled, and when the
reciprocal-tariff order of 2 April 2025 exempted gold outright the reason for it
to be there disappeared. The premium collapsed, and bullion in New York reverted
to what it had been before: inventory that earns nothing and pays storage. It
went home. Over the eight months from April to November 2025, American gold
exports averaged $10.9bn a month against $2.5bn in the baseline year, and gold
rose from the thirteenth-largest export heading to the second. Switzerland, which
had been shipping the United States $11.0bn of gold a month at the peak, was
taking $6.2bn a month back. The return was not smooth — imports jumped to
$10.7bn in July 2025 alone before falling to $1.5bn in August, and a Customs
ruling issued on 31 July that put kilo and 100-ounce bars into a dutiable heading
sits beside that spike, though the shipments precede the ruling's own date and
the timing is not yet pinned down. The round trip is the substantive point. The
same metal crossed the border twice and was counted at full value each time,
while ownership never changed hands, so anything computed from the gross
figures — a bilateral deficit, or a tariff rate calibrated on one — absorbed all
of it.

Both panels are drawn from one source: the US Census Bureau's monthly
merchandise-trade statistics, which are the customs record rather than an
estimate of it — the declared value of goods that actually crossed the border.
The series was pulled at the four-digit HS heading level, monthly, in both
directions, from October 2023 to November 2025; imports are general imports at
customs value, exports are domestic goods and re-exports together. Gold is
headings 7108 and 7115 added together, because American classification practice
has moved investment bars between the two. The left panel aggregates each heading
over all partner countries; the right panel is the same gold series broken out by
partner. The twenty-five months are grouped into three phases of twelve, five and
eight months, and every point is a monthly average within its phase, so that a
long phase cannot look large merely for being long. One caveat: these are values,
not weights, and gold averaged about a fifth dearer during the surge than in the
baseline year and about half again as dear during the reversal, so the tonnage
moved rose by less than the dollars did.

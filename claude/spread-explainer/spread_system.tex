\noindent
The spread between the COMEX gold futures price and the London benchmark is
usually reported as a single annualised number. That number mixes three
economically distinct objects: a premium for metal being in New York rather
than London, a premium for the risk that a tariff lands before delivery, and
the cost of carrying metal through time. This note writes the system down so
the three can be held apart, fixes the units, runs one real date through every
equation, states which combinations the data identify and which they do not,
sets the restrictions the system imposes against their measured values, and
lists the assumptions with the point at which each one fails.

Equation numbers match Section~9 of \path{SPREAD_EXPLAINED.html} and Section~4
of \path{spread_model_note.pdf}. Every figure quoted is printed by
\path{spread_model_checks.py}, which reads only files already in
\path{data/processed/}; its saved output is the audit trail for this document.

\label{sec:notation}

\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}

\textbf{Symbol} & \textbf{Meaning} & \textbf{Status} \\
\midrule

$F_t(\tau)$ & COMEX price for delivery in $\tau$ days & observed, 13:30 New York \\
$V_t(\tau)$ & value today of metal deliverable in New York at $\tau$ & latent \\

$h_t,\ \theta_t$ & hazard rate of a tariff; tariff rate if it lands & latent \\
$\Lambda_t(\tau)$ & probability a delivery at $\tau$ is tariffed & latent \\
$\kappa_t$ & all-in cost of moving an ounce between locations & latent; estimated \\
$\varepsilon_t$ & gold's return from 15:00 London to the settlement & latent; measurable \\

\bottomrule

\noindent\textbf{Conventions the arithmetic depends on.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=3pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Subscripts and superscripts.} $t$ indexes the trading date;
superscripts $N$ and $L$ are locations, New York and London, never powers.
\item \emph{Rates} are annualised decimals: $0.05$ is five per cent a year.
Differences between rates are quoted in percentage points (pp).
\item \emph{Day count} is 365 throughout. SOFR is quoted on a 360-day year, so
using it unadjusted overstates carry by the factor $365/360$ --- about
$0.06$~pp at 2025 rates. Not corrected; small relative to everything here.
\item \emph{$\tau$ is calendar days to first notice}, the last business day of
the month before the delivery month. First notice rather than last trade,
because from that day a holder can be handed metal, so it is the date a
financial holder must be out by.
\item \emph{Compounding.} Equations (1), (8) and the fitted curve are
continuous; (2), (12) and (13) are simple. The difference is the compounding
cross-term --- 40 cents an ounce on the worked date in \S\ref{sec:oneday}.
Mixed conventions are a real if minor inconsistency in this system.
\item \emph{$\hat p_t$ is a log difference}, $\alpha_t-\ln S_t$, so it differs
from the simple ratio $P_t/S_t$ in the third decimal: $0.473\%$ against
$0.475\%$ on the worked date. Either is fine; they should not be mixed within
a table.
\item \emph{Signs.} $p_t>0$ means New York is dear, so metal flows west;
$p_t<0$ means the reverse. A rise in the lease rate lowers the measured
dislocation.
\item \emph{Prices} are US dollars per troy ounce; flows are tonnes a month
($1$~t${}=32{,}150.7$~oz).
\end{itemize}

\section{The system}
\label{sec:system}

\noindent\textbf{Pricing through time, within New York.}
\begin{align}
F_t(\tau) &= V_t(\tau)\cdot\exp\!\left[\left(r_t+\sN-\lN\right)\frac{\tau}{365}\right]
\end{align}
A COMEX contract is metal in a New York vault at a future date, so its price is
that metal's value today compounded at the cost of holding it: interest, plus
storage, less the lease income the holder forgoes. This is the only equation
with a time dimension, and it is the gold version of covered interest parity
with $\ell^{N}$ in the role of the foreign interest rate.

\noindent\textbf{Pricing across locations, and the policy risk inside it.}
\begin{align}
V_t(\tau) &= S_t\left[\,1+p_t+\Lambda_t(\tau)\,\theta_t\,\right] \\[2pt]
\Lambda_t(\tau) &= 1-\exp\!\left(-h_t\frac{\tau}{365}\right)
   \;\approx\; h_t\frac{\tau}{365}-\frac{1}{2}\left(h_t\frac{\tau}{365}\right)^{\!2}
\end{align}
New York metal is worth the London price plus a premium. The premium has a
standing part $p_t$ --- location, bar form, and trust in a warrant versus a
claim on a bank --- and a policy part that depends on the delivery date,
because a contract delivering in nine months is likelier to be caught by a
tariff than one delivering next month. Equation (3) is where a ``level''
premium stops being a level, and it leaves two signatures: the policy term
enters the \emph{slope} of the curve, and because $\Lambda$ is concave in the
horizon it bends the curve down. The expansion needs $h_t\tau/365\ll1$; if the
hazard is large, $\Lambda$ saturates and the policy term loads on the level
instead, which turns out to matter in \S\ref{sec:ident}.

\noindent\textbf{What arbitrage enforces: a band, not a point.}
\begin{align}
-\kNL \;&\le\; p_t S_t \;\le\; \kLN
   \qquad\text{unless capacity binds} \\[2pt]
\kLN &= \underbrace{c_{\text{freight}}+c_{\text{ins}}+c_{\text{recast}}}_{\text{physical, per ounce}}
   \;+\; \underbrace{\left(r_t+\lL\right)\frac{d_{\text{transit}}}{365}\,S_t}_{\text{financing in transit}}
\end{align}
Arbitrage does not hold $p_t$ at zero; it holds it inside a band whose edges are
the cost of shipping each way. The band's width is therefore not a nuisance
parameter --- it is the transfer cost the kink regression is trying to recover.
Equation (5) is also the only place London's lease rate appears: shipping ties
up metal and money for the days in transit, and a London squeeze raises that
cost, widening the band rather than moving the COMEX curve.

\noindent\textbf{Measurement: the two legs are not observed at the same instant.}
\begin{align}
\Fhat_t(\tau) &= F_t(\tau)\cdot e^{\varepsilon_t},
   \qquad \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_t]=\mu,
   \qquad \Var[\varepsilon_t]=\sigma^{2}
\end{align}
The London benchmark is struck at 15:00 London and the COMEX settlement about
three and a half hours later, so $\varepsilon_t$ is gold's return over that
window. Every contract settles at the same instant, so $\varepsilon_t$ is
\emph{common} to all of them on date~$t$: it shifts the whole curve without
tilting it. That single property decides which results need a timing fix and
which do not.

\noindent\textbf{Flow, with a capacity ceiling.}
\begin{align}
Q_t &= \min\!\left\{\bar{Q},\;\;\beta\max\!\left(0,\;p_t S_t-\kappa_t\right)\right\}+u_t
\end{align}
Metal moves once the premium clears the cost of moving it, and no faster than
refineries and secure freight allow. The ceiling is why a premium far above the
threshold can persist for months without being an arbitrage failure.

\section{What is estimated}
\label{sec:estimated}

Taking logs of (1) and (2), substituting (3) and (6), and collecting powers of
$\tau$ gives a curve that is quadratic in the horizon. This is the regression
run each day across that date's contracts, weighted by the square root of open
interest:
\begin{align}
\ln \Fhat_t(\tau_i) &= \alpha_t+\beta_t\tau_i+\gamma_t\tau_i^{2}+\eta_{it}
\end{align}
A contract enters if $15\le\tau_i\le400$ days, open interest is at least
1{,}000 lots, and a settlement exists: under 15 days a contract is in its
delivery scramble, and far-dated or thinly held contracts carry stale
settlements. That leaves a median of six contracts a day across 2{,}857 days,
2015--2026. The three fitted coefficients map onto the objects above:
\begin{align}
\hat{p}_t \;\equiv\; \alpha_t-\ln S_t &= p_t+\varepsilon_t \\[2pt]
\hat{\omega}_t \;\equiv\; 365\beta_t-r_t &= \sN-\lN+h_t\theta_t \\[2pt]
\gamma_t &= -\,\frac{h_t^{2}\theta_t}{2\cdot 365^{2}} \;\le\; 0
\end{align}
Read them as: the intercept is a \emph{level}, in per cent of spot; the slope is
a \emph{rate}, in points a year; the curvature is a \emph{bend}, and the only
coefficient that responds to policy risk alone.

\section{The modelled spread}
\label{sec:spread}

\begin{align}
b_t(\tau)\;\equiv\;F_t(\tau)-S_t
  \;&=\; S_t\left[\;
  \underbrace{p_t}_{\substack{\text{place, form,}\\ \text{trust}}}
  \;+\;\underbrace{\Lambda_t(\tau)\,\theta_t}_{\text{policy risk}}
  \;+\;\underbrace{\left(r_t+\sN-\lN\right)\dfrac{\tau}{365}}_{\text{carry}}
  \;\right] \\[6pt]
\disloc_t(T) \;&\approx\; p_t\,\frac{365}{T}\;+\;h_t\theta_t\;+\;\left(\sN-s\right)\;-\;\lN
\end{align}

Equation (12) is the spread itself. Equation (13) is what the project's
headline number does to it: reading the fitted curve at a fixed horizon $T$ and
subtracting an assumed carry rate $s$ divides the location premium by the
horizon, leaves the policy and lease terms alone, and adds whatever error sits
in the storage assumption. The multiplier $365/T$ is $12.2$ at thirty days,
$4.06$ at ninety and $2.03$ at one hundred and eighty, so the horizon silently
sets how much of the reported number is location and how much is carry. January
2025 reads $6.90$~pp at thirty days, $2.83$ at ninety and $1.84$ at one hundred
and eighty. That is the case for reporting $\hat{p}_t$ and $\hat{\omega}_t$
separately rather than any single dislocation.

\noindent Two reference magnitudes are worth carrying: the calm-period level is
$-\$0.55$ an ounce, and the estimated flow threshold is \$3.77 an ounce with a
90\% interval of \$0.83--10.25. A one-point move in a 90-day dislocation is
therefore worth roughly twice the entire transfer cost --- which is why the
level, and not the annualised rate, is the quantity a shipping decision
responds to.

\section{Identification: what the data pin down}
\label{sec:ident}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.20\linewidth}p{0.37\linewidth}p{0.35\linewidth}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Object} & \textbf{Identified by} & \textbf{Caveat} \\
\midrule
$p_t$ & the intercept, $\alpha_t-\ln S_t$ & carries $\varepsilon_t$; a monthly mean has reliability $0.80$ \\
$\sN-\lN$ & the slope, $365\beta_t-r_t$ & not separable without an external storage or lease quote \\
$h_t\theta_t$ & the slope in excess of a calm baseline & only as good as that baseline \\
$h_t$, $\theta_t$ apart & slope together with curvature & rejected in practice; see below \\
$\kappa_t$ & the kink in (7), and the calm band of $p_tS_t$ & wide interval, \$0.83--10.25 \\
$\varepsilon_t$ & intraday COMEX prices at the auction instant & not on disk; one Databento job \\
place / form / trust & \textit{not identified} & needs warrant- or bar-level prices \\
$\lL$ & \textit{not identified from this curve} & enters only through $\kappa_t$ in (5) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent\textbf{The hazard and the tariff, in principle.} Equations (10) and
(11) are two moments in two unknowns, once a baseline for $\sN-\lN$ is fixed.
Writing $A\equiv h_t\theta_t$ from the slope and
$B\equiv-2\cdot365^{2}\gamma_t$ from the curvature,
\[
h_t \;=\; \frac{B}{A}, \qquad\qquad \theta_t \;=\; \frac{A^{2}}{B}.
\]

\noindent\textbf{In practice it is rejected, informatively.} Taking the calm
years 2015--19 as the baseline ($\hat\omega=0.58\%$,
$\gamma=-0.007\times10^{-6}$):

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=4pt]
\item For January 2025, $\hat\omega=+0.975$~pp gives $A=0.00395$ a year. The
curvature then implies $h_t\approx3.4$--$3.9$ a year --- a 57--62\% chance of a
tariff within ninety days --- paired with $\theta_t\approx0.10$--$0.12\%$. A
near-certain tariff of one tenth of one per cent is not a description of what
was being discussed, so the over-identifying restriction fails. The curvature
is mostly ordinary term structure: it is already negative in the calm years.
\item Run the other way, the slope \emph{bounds} the risk-neutral tariff
expectation, and the bound is small. Imposing a 10\% chance within ninety days
gives $\theta_t=0.92\%$; a 25\% chance gives $0.34\%$; a 50\% chance gives
$0.14\%$. Against a tariff under discussion at 10--39\%, the term structure was
pricing almost none of it.
\item Across the whole episode window, November 2024 to April 2025,
$\hat\omega$ averaged $+0.561$~pp --- \emph{below} the calm baseline --- so
$A<0$ and the slope prices no positive hazard at all.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The reading is not that the tariff scare was imaginary but that it was
priced as a \emph{level} on metal already in New York rather than as a term
premium on future delivery. That is consistent with the level accounting for
roughly 85\% of the January 2025 move once the five-year calm mean rather than
a single calm day is used as the counterfactual. Equation (3) earns its place
as a restriction the data reject, not as a channel they support.

\noindent One further restriction is not a test but an arithmetic identity, and
it fails. The calm-period slope gives $\sN-\lN=+0.58$~pp a year. With
$\ell^{N}\approx0$ in calm years that implies storage of about $0.58\%$, not
the $0.25\%$ assumed; if storage really is $0.25\%$, the implied lease rate is
$-0.33\%$, which nobody quotes. One of the two is mis-set, and because $s$
enters (13) one-for-one, so is every reported dislocation \emph{level}. Read
levels in differences until this is resolved.

\section{Assumptions, and where each one fails}
\label{sec:assume}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=4pt]
\item \textbf{Futures are treated as forwards.} Daily margining makes a futures
price differ from a forward when interest rates covary with the gold price. The
wedge is small at these horizons, but it lands in $\beta_t$ --- which is where
the lease rate is read.
\item \textbf{One representative New York price.} Registered and eligible
warrants, and 100-oz against kilo bars, are all folded into $p_t$. The
registered/eligible split is itself a signal the project tracks separately.
\item \textbf{One lease rate per location, flat across $\tau$.} If the lease
curve is sloped, part of it appears as curvature and contaminates
\S\ref{sec:ident}.
\item \textbf{The curve is log-quadratic.} A third-order term would reallocate
weight between $\beta_t$ and $\gamma_t$, which is precisely the margin the
hazard identification rests on.
\item \textbf{$\sqrt{\text{open interest}}$ weights are ad hoc.} The defensible
weight is inverse settlement-error variance, for which open interest is a proxy
of unknown shape. In practice the heaviest contract carries a median $46\%$ of
the weight (middle half $43$--$51\%$), so the ``constant maturity'' curve is
less independent of the front month than the name suggests.
\item \textbf{$\varepsilon_t$ is assumed mean zero.} Only its variance has been
measured. The drift lower bound is $\$0.31$ an ounce: negligible against a \$14
episode premium, not negligible against a $-\$0.55$ calm mean. One intraday
pull settles it.
\item \textbf{(7) is estimated on monthly data.} Shipments respond to the
premium and also close it, so the coefficient is an equilibrium relation rather
than the supply of arbitrage. It is descriptive; identification needs
within-month timing or an event study.
\item \textbf{Swiss customs is a partial flow measure.} It misses direct
London--New York shipments of already-deliverable bars, and non-Swiss origins.
If the missing flows respond to the same signal, $\beta$ is attenuated and
$\kappa_t$ biased.
\end{itemize}

\section{How to use it}
\label{sec:use}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=4pt]
\item \textbf{Report $\hat p_t$ in dollars an ounce and $\hat\omega_t$ in points
a year.} No horizon, no annualising of a level, and both are directly
comparable to quantities measured outside this project: transfer costs for one,
lease rates for the other.
\item \textbf{Re-time the New York leg} with an \path{ohlcv-1m} pull priced at
the London auction instant, then re-estimate. Until then, divide kink slopes by
the $0.80$ reliability.
\item \textbf{Pin down storage.} A quoted vault-and-insurance schedule breaks
the $\sN$ versus $\ell^{N}$ tie and fixes every level in the project at once.
\item \textbf{Test whether the premium is a level or a risk} by reading the
fitted curve at 30, 90 and 180 days and comparing $p_t/S_t$ across them: equal
means a level, rising means a horizon-dependent risk.
\item \textbf{Use the dated resolutions as identification.} At resolution
$h_t\to0$, so the slope and curvature should step at the April 2025 exemption
and the July 2025 customs ruling while the level does not.
\item \textbf{Re-specify (7) with the ceiling}, and say plainly that the
coefficient is descriptive.
\end{enumerate}

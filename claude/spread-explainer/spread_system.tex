\begin{abstract}
\noindent
The difference between the COMEX gold futures price and the London benchmark
is conventionally summarised as a single annualised rate. We show that this
statistic is a horizon-weighted mixture of two economically distinct objects
--- a premium for metal being located in New York, which is a level, and a
wedge between storage costs and the lease rate, which is a rate --- and that
the weight on the first, $365/T$, is set by the horizon at which the analyst
chooses to read the futures curve. Estimating the curve daily across contracts
over 2015--2026 separates the two, and three results follow. First, the
measurement error induced by the three-and-a-half-hour gap between the London
auction and the COMEX settlement is common across contracts and therefore
loads entirely on the curve's intercept: a regression of the estimated level on
the day's return yields $0.193$ against the $0.146$ implied by a random walk
over the window, while the same regression on the slope yields $-0.012$ and is
indistinguishable from zero. Second, the location premium averages $-\$0.55$ an
ounce in calm years, inside a transfer-cost band independently estimated at
\$0.83--10.25, whereas the calm-period slope implies storage net of lease
income of $0.58$ percentage points a year --- inconsistent with the $0.25\%$
storage cost assumed throughout this literature. Third, the 2024--25 United
States gold tariff episode was priced almost entirely as a level: the slope
bounds the risk-neutral expected tariff at $0.92\%$ even under a ten per cent
arrival probability, and an over-identifying restriction linking slope and
curvature to a tariff hazard is rejected.

\vspace{0.6em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} gold, commodity basis, covered interest parity,
limits to arbitrage, trade policy, term structure.

\noindent\textbf{JEL:} G13, G14, F13, C22.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Gold is traded simultaneously in two locations with different market
conventions: in London as unallocated claims on metal held in vaults, and in
New York as exchange-warranted metal deliverable against a futures contract.
The difference between the two prices is the object that determines whether
metal physically crosses the Atlantic, and it is therefore the price signal
behind episodes in which very large gross bilateral flows appear in trade
statistics without any corresponding change in ownership or consumption. The
2024--25 episode, in which roughly 535 tonnes moved from Switzerland to the
United States and subsequently returned, is the motivating case.

That difference is almost always reported as an annualised rate, the
``dislocation'': the futures-to-spot ratio converted to an implied financing
rate and netted of a benchmark carry rate. This paper argues that the statistic
is not a well-defined economic object. Writing the basis as the sum of a
location premium, a policy-risk premium and a carry term makes the problem
visible: annualising divides the first component by the horizon at which the
curve is read while leaving the third unchanged, so the reported number mixes a
level and a rate in proportions the analyst fixes by choosing a horizon. At
thirty days the weight on the location premium is $12.2$; at ninety it is
$4.06$; at one hundred and eighty it is $2.03$. January 2025 reads $6.90$,
$2.83$ and $1.84$ percentage points at those three horizons respectively, from
identical underlying data.

The alternative pursued here is to estimate the futures curve directly. On each
date we fit the log settlement price across the traded contracts as a quadratic
function of time to first notice, weighting by the square root of open
interest. The intercept, net of the contemporaneous London price, estimates the
location premium as a level. The slope, net of the dollar interest rate,
estimates storage costs net of the lease rate. The curvature responds only to
horizon-dependent policy risk. The decomposition requires no horizon
convention, and its components are separately interpretable: the first is
comparable to freight and refining costs, the second to quoted lease rates.

Four findings follow. First, the measurement error created by observing the two
legs at different instants is a single common shift in every contract's log
price on a given date, so it enters the intercept and not the slope
(Proposition~\ref{prop:timing}). This is testable and is borne out: the
estimated level covaries with the day's gold return with a coefficient of
$0.193$ (standard error $0.015$) against a random-walk benchmark of
$3.5/24=0.146$, whereas the slope does not covary with it at all, $-0.012$
(standard error $0.011$). Results resting on the slope therefore require no
timing correction, and results resting on the level can be corrected by a
reliability ratio of $0.80$ at monthly frequency.

Second, in the calm years 2015--19 the estimated location premium averages
$-\$0.55$ an ounce, or $-0.047\%$ of spot. Arbitrage thus holds the premium
inside a narrow band around zero, as the theory requires, and the band's width
is the transfer cost the threshold regressions in this literature attempt to
recover; a threshold estimated from physical flows puts it at \$3.77 an ounce
with a ninety per cent interval of \$0.83--10.25, and trade sources put the
all-in cost of moving London metal into COMEX-deliverable form at \$1.5--2.5.
The three readings are mutually consistent.

Third, the same calm period delivers a slope implying storage net of lease
income of $0.58$ percentage points a year. If the New York lease rate is near
zero when nothing is happening, storage is of the order of $0.58\%$, not the
$0.25\%$ conventionally assumed; if storage is $0.25\%$, the implied lease rate
is $-0.33\%$, which is not a rate that is quoted. Because the assumed storage
cost enters the annualised dislocation one-for-one, every reported
\emph{level} of that statistic inherits the error, and only differences are
safe to interpret.

Fourth, the tariff episode was priced as a level on metal already inside the
United States rather than as a term premium on future delivery. Inverting the
slope for the risk-neutral expected tariff gives $0.92\%$ under a ten per cent
arrival probability within ninety days, $0.34\%$ under twenty-five and $0.14\%$
under fifty, against a tariff under public discussion at ten to thirty-nine per
cent. The over-identifying restriction that links the slope and the curvature
to a common hazard is rejected: it implies a near-certain tariff of one tenth
of one per cent.

\paragraph{Related literature.}
The framework is the theory of storage (Kaldor, 1939; Working, 1949; Fama and
French, 1987) applied to a commodity for which the convenience yield is
unusually well identified, because above-ground inventories are enormous
relative to flow demand and the own-rate of interest is directly quoted as a
lease rate. Treating the futures price as a forward price abstracts from the
margining wedge analysed by Cox, Ingersoll and Ross (1981). The location
premium is the commodity analogue of a covered interest parity deviation, and
the mechanism that sustains it --- balance-sheet and financing costs borne by
constrained intermediaries --- is the one emphasised in the currency literature
by Du, Tepper and Verdelhan (2018) and Avdjiev, Du, Koch and Shin (2019), and
more generally by Shleifer and Vishny (1997). The band structure in which
arbitrage operates only beyond a transfer cost, and the resulting threshold in
the flow response, is estimated in the manner of Hansen (2000). The relation
between cash and futures prices as a price-discovery problem across venues
follows Garbade and Silber (1983).

\section{Institutional setting and data}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{The London leg.}
The London Bullion Market Association Gold Price is set by auction, administered
by ICE Benchmark Administration, twice daily; we use the afternoon auction,
struck at 15:00 London time, in US dollars per troy ounce. The price refers to
unallocated metal held in London vaults --- a claim on a bullion bank's pool
rather than title to identified bars --- and is retrieved from the LBMA's
public price service.

\paragraph{The New York leg.}
The COMEX gold futures contract calls for delivery of 100 troy ounces of metal
in an exchange-approved vault in the New York area, conveyed as a warrant.
Contracts are listed for a range of delivery months, of which February, April,
June, August, October and December carry the great majority of open interest.
For each contract and date we use the official settlement price, computed by
the exchange from trading around 13:30 New York time, together with open
interest. Both are taken from Databento's \path{GLBX.MDP3} feed: settlement is
statistic type~3 and open interest type~9. Settlement rather than closing price
is the relevant quotation because it is the price at which positions are
marked and against which an exchange-for-physical transaction is struck.

\paragraph{Rates and storage.}
The dollar interest rate is the Secured Overnight Financing Rate from 3 April
2018, spliced with the effective federal funds rate before that date, both from
FRED. Storage is set to a constant $0.25\%$ a year, an assumption rather than
an observation: no free public series for vaulting and insurance exists.
Section~\ref{sec:results} shows the data reject this value.

\paragraph{Horizon and contract selection.}
Time to delivery is measured as $\tau$, calendar days to first notice day,
defined as the last business day of the month preceding the delivery month.
First notice rather than last trade is the economically relevant horizon
because from that date a long position may be assigned metal, so a financial
holder must close or roll before it. A contract enters the daily cross-section
if $15\le\tau\le400$ days, open interest is at least 1{,}000 lots, and a
settlement price exists: inside fifteen days a contract is in its delivery
window and no longer behaves as a forward, while far-dated or thinly held
contracts carry stale settlements. This leaves a median of six contracts a day.
Requiring both markets to be open loses 178 of 3{,}035 weekdays, predominantly
around Christmas, New Year, Independence Day and Juneteenth. The estimation
sample is 2{,}857 trading days, 2 January 2015 to 20 August 2026.

\section{The model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Carry within a location}

A futures contract on metal held in New York is priced by the cost of carrying
that metal to the delivery date. Writing $V_t(\tau)$ for the value today of
metal deliverable in New York at horizon $\tau$,
\begin{align}
F_t(\tau) &= V_t(\tau)\cdot\exp\!\left[\left(r_t+\sN-\lN\right)\frac{\tau}{365}\right].
\end{align}
The holder finances the position at $r_t$, pays storage $\sN$, and forgoes the
lease income $\lN$ that metal in hand could earn if lent. Equation (1) is the
gold analogue of covered interest parity, with the lease rate in the role of
the foreign interest rate; it is the only equation in the system with a time
dimension.

\subsection{The location wedge and policy risk}

Metal in New York need not be worth metal in London. Let $p_t$ denote the
premium on New York metal as a fraction of the London price, comprising
location, bar form --- London trades 400-ounce bars, COMEX takes 100-ounce and
kilogramme bars --- and the difference between an exchange warrant and a claim
on a bank. Suppose in addition that an import tariff at rate $\theta_t$ may be
imposed, arriving with constant hazard $h_t$, and that metal already inside the
United States is exempt while metal imported for later delivery is not. Then
\begin{align}
V_t(\tau) &= S_t\left[\,1+p_t+\Lambda_t(\tau)\,\theta_t\,\right] \\[2pt]
\Lambda_t(\tau) &= 1-\exp\!\left(-h_t\frac{\tau}{365}\right)
   \;\approx\; h_t\frac{\tau}{365}-\frac{1}{2}\left(h_t\frac{\tau}{365}\right)^{\!2},
\end{align}
where $\Lambda_t(\tau)$ is the risk-neutral probability that a delivery at
$\tau$ is caught by the tariff. Equation (3) is the point at which the premium
ceases to be a pure level: the expected tariff cost rises with the horizon, so
policy risk enters the \emph{slope} of the curve, and because $\Lambda_t$ is
concave in $\tau$ it also bends the curve downwards. The expansion requires
$h_t\tau/365\ll1$; if the hazard is large, $\Lambda_t$ saturates and the policy
term reverts to a level, a case Section~\ref{sec:results} returns to.

\subsection{The arbitrage band and the transfer cost}

Arbitrage does not force $p_t$ to zero. It confines it to a band whose edges
are the cost of moving metal in each direction,
\begin{align}
-\kNL \;&\le\; p_t S_t \;\le\; \kLN
   \qquad\text{unless capacity binds} \\[2pt]
\kLN &= \underbrace{c_{\text{freight}}+c_{\text{ins}}+c_{\text{recast}}}_{\text{physical, per ounce}}
   \;+\; \underbrace{\left(r_t+\lL\right)\frac{d_{\text{transit}}}{365}\,S_t}_{\text{financing in transit}} .
\end{align}
The width of the band is therefore not a nuisance parameter but the quantity
that the threshold in the flow equation identifies. Equation (5) is also the
only point at which the \emph{London} lease rate enters the system: shipping
immobilises metal and money for the days in transit, so a London squeeze raises
the cost of the arbitrage and widens the band, without shifting the COMEX
curve. A single lease rate, as in (1), is therefore an approximation that fails
precisely when the two markets are segmented.

\subsection{Measurement}

The two legs are not observed at the same instant. The London benchmark is
struck at 15:00 London and the COMEX settlement approximately three and a half
hours later, so the observed futures price embeds gold's return over the
intervening window,
\begin{align}
\Fhat_t(\tau) &= F_t(\tau)\cdot e^{\varepsilon_t},
   \qquad \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_t]=\mu,
   \qquad \Var[\varepsilon_t]=\sigma^{2}.
\end{align}
Because every contract settles at the same instant, $\varepsilon_t$ is common
across the cross-section on date $t$. This is the property that makes the
measurement problem tractable, and Proposition~\ref{prop:timing} states its
consequence.

\subsection{Flows}

Metal moves when the premium exceeds the cost of moving it, subject to the
capacity of refineries and secure freight,
\begin{align}
Q_t &= \min\!\left\{\bar{Q},\;\;\beta\max\!\left(0,\;p_t S_t-\kappa_t\right)\right\}+u_t .
\end{align}
The ceiling matters for interpretation: a premium far above the threshold can
persist for months without constituting an arbitrage failure, because the
technology for closing it is rate-limited.

\section{Estimation and identification}
\label{sec:ident}

Taking logarithms of (1) and (2), substituting (3) and (6) and collecting powers
of $\tau$ (Appendix~\ref{app:derivation}) yields a curve that is quadratic in
the horizon. The estimating equation, fitted by weighted least squares across
the contracts available on each date with weights equal to the square root of
open interest, is
\begin{align}
\ln \Fhat_t(\tau_i) &= \alpha_t+\beta_t\tau_i+\gamma_t\tau_i^{2}+\eta_{it},
\end{align}
and the three coefficients map onto the objects of Section~\ref{sec:model} as
\begin{align}
\hat{p}_t \;\equiv\; \alpha_t-\ln S_t &= p_t+\varepsilon_t \\[2pt]
\hat{\omega}_t \;\equiv\; 365\beta_t-r_t &= \sN-\lN+h_t\theta_t \\[2pt]
\gamma_t &= -\,\frac{h_t^{2}\theta_t}{2\cdot 365^{2}} \;\le\; 0 .
\end{align}
The intercept is a level, measured in per cent of spot; the slope is a rate,
measured in percentage points a year; the curvature is a bend, and is the only
coefficient that responds to policy risk alone. Reassembling the components
gives the spread at any horizon and, as a special case, the conventional
statistic:
\begin{align}
b_t(\tau)\;\equiv\;F_t(\tau)-S_t
  \;&=\; S_t\left[\;
  \underbrace{p_t}_{\substack{\text{place, form,}\\ \text{trust}}}
  \;+\;\underbrace{\Lambda_t(\tau)\,\theta_t}_{\text{policy risk}}
  \;+\;\underbrace{\left(r_t+\sN-\lN\right)\dfrac{\tau}{365}}_{\text{carry}}
  \;\right] \\[6pt]
\disloc_t(T) \;&\approx\; p_t\,\frac{365}{T}\;+\;h_t\theta_t\;+\;\left(\sN-s\right)\;-\;\lN .
\end{align}

Equation (13) is the paper's first point. Reading the fitted curve at a fixed
horizon $T$ and subtracting an assumed carry rate $s$ divides the location
premium by the horizon, leaves the policy and lease terms untouched, and adds
the error in the storage assumption. The statistic is therefore a
horizon-weighted mixture whose composition the analyst selects, not a
parameter.

\begin{proposition}[Measurement error loads on the level]
\label{prop:timing}
If $\varepsilon_t$ is common across contracts on date $t$, then it enters the
intercept of (8) one-for-one and does not enter the slope or the curvature.
Consequently $\hat{p}_t$ is measured with error while $\hat{\omega}_t$ and
$\gamma_t$ are not, and $\mathrm{plim}\,\mathrm{cov}(\hat{\omega}_t,
\varepsilon_t)=0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[What the curve identifies]
\label{prop:ident}
From the daily cross-section alone: $p_t$ is identified up to the additive
measurement error $\varepsilon_t$; the combination $\sN-\lN$ is identified
whenever $h_t\theta_t=0$; and the product $h_t\theta_t$ is identified relative
to a period in which it is known to vanish. Neither $\sN$ nor $\lN$ is
separately identified, the decomposition of $p_t$ into location, form and trust
components is not identified, and $\lL$ does not enter the cross-section at all.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Over-identification of the hazard]
\label{prop:hazard}
Given a baseline value for $\sN-\lN$, equations (10) and (11) constitute two
moments in the two unknowns $h_t$ and $\theta_t$. Writing $A\equiv h_t\theta_t$
and $B\equiv-2\cdot365^{2}\gamma_t$,
\begin{equation*}
h_t=\frac{B}{A}, \qquad \theta_t=\frac{A^{2}}{B},
\end{equation*}
so the model is over-identified: any third moment, or an independent estimate
of either parameter, constitutes a test.
\end{proposition}

Proofs are in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.

\subsection{Scaling}

Because the components are measured in different units, comparisons require
explicit conversion, and Table~\ref{tab:units} provides it. One percentage
point of the conventional dislocation corresponds to $0.0822\%$ of spot at a
thirty-day horizon and $0.4932\%$ at one hundred and eighty; at a gold price of
\$2{,}756 those are \$2.27 and \$13.59 an ounce respectively. Conversely, a
transfer cost of \$2 an ounce is $0.0726\%$ of spot, equivalent to $0.29$
percentage points when read at ninety days. A one-point movement in a
ninety-day dislocation is thus worth roughly twice the entire cost of shipping
the metal, which is the practical reason for preferring the level to the rate
when the object of interest is a shipping decision.

in three episodes: the spring of 2020, when freight and refining capacity
collapsed; January 2025, during the tariff anticipation; and the late summer of
2025, following the customs ruling that reclassified bullion. The carry wedge
moves on an entirely different rhythm, peaking in mid-2020 at roughly three
percentage points and drifting through 2025 without a marked episode. That the
two series are only loosely related is the empirical content of the
decomposition: a single annualised statistic necessarily blends them.

\subsection{Testing the measurement model}

Proposition~\ref{prop:timing} has a direct empirical implication, reported in
Table~\ref{tab:timing}. Regressing the estimated level on the contemporaneous
London-to-London return yields a coefficient of $0.193$, close to but somewhat
above the $0.146$ that a random walk over three and a half of twenty-four hours
implies; the excess is what one expects if the United States morning carries
more than a time-proportional share of the day's variance. The same regression
for the slope yields $-0.012$ with a standard error of $0.011$, which is the
prediction of the proposition. A placebo using the previous day's return
returns $0.049$ (standard error $0.017$), small but not zero, indicating mild
persistence that a purely classical error model does not capture.

Scaling up, the daily standard deviation of the London benchmark is $1.01\%$,
implying $0.39\%$ of spot, or \$7.44 an ounce, of measurement noise in a single
day's level; the measured standard deviation of the level is $0.50\%$, so the
timing gap accounts for roughly sixty per cent of its daily variance and
forty-seven per cent in 2019 alone. At monthly frequency, averaging twenty
sessions reduces the noise to $0.086\%$ against a monthly series standard
deviation of $0.192\%$, a reliability ratio of $0.80$: slope coefficients
estimated on the monthly level are attenuated by a fifth and should be scaled
by $1.25$. The bias is bounded below by \$0.31 an ounce under a uniform-drift
assumption, which is negligible relative to an episode premium of \$14 but not
relative to a calm mean of $-\$0.55$. Re-timing the New York leg using intraday
prices at the auction instant would eliminate the problem rather than
correcting for it.

\subsection{Storage and the lease rate}

The calm-period slope implies $\sN-\lN=+0.58$ percentage points a year. The two
cannot be separated without external information (Proposition~\ref{prop:ident}),
but neither admissible resolution is comfortable. If the New York lease rate is
approximately zero when the market is quiet, storage is of the order of
$0.58\%$ a year rather than the $0.25\%$ assumed; if storage is indeed
$0.25\%$, the lease rate implied is $-0.33\%$, which is not a rate that is
quoted in this market. Because the assumed storage cost enters (13) one-for-one,
the level of every reported dislocation is displaced by the error. Differences
are unaffected, and until an external vaulting and insurance schedule is
obtained, only differences should be interpreted.

\subsection{Was the tariff episode priced in the term structure?}

Proposition~\ref{prop:hazard} allows the hazard and the tariff rate to be
recovered separately, given a baseline for $\sN-\lN$. Using 2015--19 as the
baseline ($\hat{\omega}=0.58\%$, $\gamma=-0.007\times10^{-6}$), January 2025
delivers $A=h_t\theta_t=0.00395$ a year. Combining this with the observed
curvature yields $h_t$ between $3.4$ and $3.9$ a year --- a fifty-seven to
sixty-two per cent probability of imposition within ninety days --- paired with
$\theta_t$ between $0.10$ and $0.12$ per cent. A near-certain tariff of one
tenth of one per cent does not describe the policy under discussion, and the
over-identifying restriction is therefore rejected. The natural reading is that
the curvature is dominated by ordinary term-structure variation rather than by
a policy hazard: it is already negative in the calm years, where by
construction no tariff risk exists.

Imposing a hazard instead and inverting (10) for the tariff rate bounds the
risk-neutral expectation, and the bound is small: a ten per cent probability
within ninety days implies $\theta_t=0.92\%$, twenty-five per cent implies
$0.34\%$, and fifty per cent implies $0.14\%$. Over the full anticipation
window, November 2024 to April 2025, $\hat{\omega}$ averages $+0.561$
percentage points, \emph{below} the calm baseline, so the slope prices no
positive hazard at all. Against a tariff under public discussion at ten to
thirty-nine per cent, the term structure was pricing almost none of it.

The conclusion is not that the episode was imaginary but that it was priced as
a level on metal already inside the United States rather than as a term premium
on future delivery. This is consistent with the accounting in
Table~\ref{tab:periods}: measured against the five-year calm mean rather than a
single quiet day, the level accounts for approximately eighty-five per cent of
the January 2025 movement in the conventional statistic.

\subsection{Flows}

The threshold in (7), estimated on monthly Swiss customs shipments to the
United States over 2023--26, is \$3.77 an ounce with a ninety per cent interval
of \$0.83--10.25; below it, flows sit on a floor of two to three tonnes a
month, and in the highest premium bucket the median is sixty-four tonnes. The
point estimate is consistent both with the calm-period location premium and
with trade estimates of \$1.5--2.5 an ounce for freight, insurance and
recasting, which is the joint validation the band in (4) makes available. The
coefficient itself, however, is not structural: shipments respond to the
premium and simultaneously close it, so a monthly regression recovers an
equilibrium relation rather than the supply of arbitrage.

\section{Specification and limitations}
\label{sec:robust}

\paragraph{The reading horizon.}
Although the decomposition requires no horizon, the conventional statistic
does, and the choice is not innocuous. Three criteria are measurable. First,
support: the shortest contract in the daily cross-section is a median of thirty
days out and at most seventy-seven, so a horizon of seventy-seven days or more
lies inside the observed data on every date in the sample, whereas a thirty-day
reading extrapolates below the shortest available contract on $48.3\%$ of
dates. Second, precision: the weighted centre of mass of the cross-section
averages eighty-two days, and on a grid from twenty to two hundred and seventy
days the variance of the fitted value is minimised at ninety. Third,
specification: allowing the quadratic term moves the fitted value least, in
dollar terms, near ninety days (\$0.33 an ounce, against \$0.46 at thirty and
\$0.40 at one hundred and eighty). Ninety days is also the tenor at which the
covered interest parity literature conventionally quotes deviations. These
criteria justify the convention without rescuing the statistic.

\paragraph{Weighting.}
Square-root-of-open-interest weights are ad hoc; the defensible weight is the
inverse variance of the settlement error, for which open interest is a proxy of
unknown functional form. In practice the most heavily weighted contract carries
a median $46\%$ of the total weight, with a middle half of $43$--$51\%$, so the
fitted curve is less independent of the front contract than the term
``constant maturity'' suggests.

\paragraph{Futures against forwards.}
Equation (1) treats the futures price as a forward price, abstracting from the
margining wedge of Cox, Ingersoll and Ross (1981). The wedge is small at these
horizons but falls on $\beta_t$, which is where the lease rate is read.

\paragraph{Aggregation within a location.}
The model admits one New York price, so the registered and eligible warrant
categories, and the difference between 100-ounce and kilogramme bars, are
absorbed into $p_t$. A single lease rate per location, flat in $\tau$, is
likewise an approximation; a sloped lease curve would appear as curvature and
contaminate Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\paragraph{Functional form.}
A third-order term in (8) would reallocate weight between $\beta_t$ and
$\gamma_t$, which is precisely the margin on which
Proposition~\ref{prop:hazard} is tested. The rejection reported above should be
read as a rejection of the joint hypothesis that the curve is log-quadratic and
that its curvature is generated by a tariff hazard.

\paragraph{The flow measure.}
Swiss customs shipments omit direct London-to-New York movements of
already-deliverable bars, and non-Swiss origins. If the omitted flows respond
to the same signal, $\beta$ is attenuated and the estimated threshold biased.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The conventional annualised gold basis is a mixture of a level and a rate whose
composition is determined by an analytical choice rather than by the market.
Estimating the futures curve directly separates the two at no cost in data: the
intercept identifies a location premium comparable to physical transfer costs,
and the slope identifies storage net of the lease rate, comparable to quoted
lease rates. Doing so shows that the measurement error induced by
non-synchronous observation is confined to the level, that the storage cost
assumed in this literature is inconsistent with the term structure in calm
periods, and that the 2024--25 tariff episode was a level phenomenon that the
term structure barely priced.

Three extensions would sharpen the analysis at low cost. Re-timing the New York
leg with intraday prices at the auction instant would remove rather than
attenuate the measurement error. An external vaulting and insurance schedule
would break the storage-lease identification problem and thereby fix the level
of every dislocation series in this literature. Finally, the dated resolutions
of the policy episode --- the April 2025 exemption and the July 2025 customs
ruling --- provide events at which the hazard falls to zero by construction,
and hence a sharper test of whether policy risk ever entered the slope than the
period comparisons used here.

\section{Derivation of the estimating equation}
\label{app:derivation}

Substituting (2) into (1), multiplying by the measurement factor in (6) and
taking logarithms,
\begin{equation}
\ln \Fhat_t(\tau) = \ln S_t + \ln\!\left[1+p_t+\Lambda_t(\tau)\theta_t\right]
  + \left(r_t+\sN-\lN\right)\frac{\tau}{365} + \varepsilon_t .
\end{equation}
For small $p_t$ and $\Lambda_t\theta_t$ the logarithm is approximately its
argument less one, and substituting the second-order expansion of $\Lambda_t$
from (3) gives
\begin{equation}
\ln \Fhat_t(\tau) \approx \underbrace{\ln S_t + p_t + \varepsilon_t}_{\alpha_t}
  + \underbrace{\left[\frac{r_t+\sN-\lN}{365} + \frac{h_t\theta_t}{365}\right]}_{\beta_t}\tau
  - \underbrace{\frac{h_t^{2}\theta_t}{2\cdot365^{2}}}_{-\gamma_t}\,\tau^{2},
\end{equation}
which is (8) with the coefficient mappings (9), (10) and (11). Two features are
worth noting. The location premium and the measurement error are
indistinguishable within a date because both are constants in $\tau$; and the
policy term contributes to both the linear and quadratic coefficients, which is
what makes Proposition~\ref{prop:hazard} available.

\section{Proofs}
\label{app:proofs}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:timing}]
By (6) the observed log price on date $t$ is $\ln F_t(\tau_i)+\varepsilon_t$
for every $i$ in the cross-section, so $\varepsilon_t$ is collinear with the
constant regressor in (8) and orthogonal to $\tau_i$ and $\tau_i^{2}$ within
the date. Weighted least squares therefore attributes it entirely to
$\alpha_t$, leaving $\beta_t$ and $\gamma_t$ unchanged. Since $\hat{\omega}_t$
is a function of $\beta_t$ and the observed $r_t$ alone, it is uncorrelated
with $\varepsilon_t$ by construction.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:ident}]
Immediate from (9), (10) and (11). Equation (9) gives $p_t+\varepsilon_t$, so
$p_t$ is identified only up to the error, and consistently under averaging if
$\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_t]=0$. Equation (10) gives the sum
$\sN-\lN+h_t\theta_t$; with $h_t\theta_t=0$ it gives $\sN-\lN$, but no function
of the cross-section separates $\sN$ from $\lN$, since both enter only through
that sum. Given a period in which $h_t\theta_t=0$, the difference of (10)
between periods identifies $h_t\theta_t$ under the assumption that $\sN-\lN$ is
unchanged. The three components of $p_t$ enter (2) only through their sum. The
London lease rate $\lL$ appears in (5) alone, which involves no quantity in the
New York cross-section.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:hazard}]
Write $A=h_t\theta_t$ from (10) net of the baseline and
$B=-2\cdot365^{2}\gamma_t=h_t^{2}\theta_t$ from (11). Then $B/A=h_t$ and
$A/h_t=A^{2}/B=\theta_t$, provided $A\neq0$ and $B>0$. Both moments derive from
the same fitted curve, so any additional restriction --- a third moment, an
independent estimate of $h_t$ or $\theta_t$, or a sign restriction such as
$\theta_t$ lying in a plausible range --- is testable.
\end{proof}

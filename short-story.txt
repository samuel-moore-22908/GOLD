The Assay

Mara had panned six creeks before she found color that meant anything. Most days the pan gave her nothing but mica, flecks that glittered and lied. This flake sat heavier, sank faster, refused to wash out with the silt no matter how she tilted the pan or let the current argue with her wrist.

She carried it two days to the assayer's shack at the fork, walking past claims abandoned so long the tent poles had rotted into the ground and the stakes had gone soft and gray as driftwood. The trail there was mud, then ice at the edges of the mud, then mud again where the sun found a gap in the pines. She didn't stop to eat. Hunger made the walking easier, gave her something simple to argue with instead of the cold.

The assayer was a thin man named Osei, new to the fork that season, though people already spoke of him like he'd always been there. He weighed the flake on a scale he kept under glass, out of the wind and dust that came through the door with every customer. He didn't speak while he worked, and Mara had learned not to fill silences with men like him — men who treated quiet as a kind of honesty, and words, when they came, as currency they didn't spend lightly.

"Twenty-two carat," he said finally, setting the flake down with two fingers, as if it might still get away from him. "Maybe better."

She asked what that meant, in money.

He told her, and the number sat wrong in her chest — too small for six weeks of frozen hands and a knee that had stopped bending right in the mornings, too large for a single flake of metal small enough to lose in a coat seam. He asked where she'd found it. She didn't answer. Upstream was hers until someone else proved otherwise, and out here, proof took longer than most claims survived.

Osei slid the flake back across the counter. "Come back with more," he said, "and I'll believe you."

She almost laughed at that. Belief had never once put color in a pan.

She walked out into weather that had already turned the mud to ice at its edges, the sky the color of the underside of a fish. She thought not of the creek but of the way he'd said believe — like gold was a story you had to keep telling, one flake at a time, until someone else agreed it was true. As if the creek cared whether it was believed. As if six weeks of kneeling in freezing water were an argument and not just a fact she'd lived through.

Six weeks was nothing, she told herself. She had time, and upstream, and a knee that would hold for one more season if she asked it to.

She turned toward the trail before the light could go, and did not look back at the shack.

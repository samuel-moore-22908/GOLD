The Assay

Mara had panned six creeks before she found color that meant anything. Most days the pan gave her nothing but mica, flecks that glittered and lied. This flake sat heavier, sank faster, refused to wash out with the silt no matter how she tilted the water.

She carried it two days to the assayer's shack at the fork, past claims abandoned so long the tent poles had rotted into the ground. The assayer, a thin man named Osei, weighed the flake on a scale he kept under glass. He didn't speak while he worked, and Mara had learned not to fill silences with men like him.

"Twenty-two carat," he said finally. "Maybe better."

She asked what that meant, in money.

He told her, and the number sat wrong in her chest — too small for six weeks of frozen hands, too large for a single flake of metal. He asked where she'd found it. She didn't answer. Upstream was hers until someone else proved otherwise, and out here, proof took longer than most claims survived.

Osei slid the flake back across the counter. "Come back with more," he said, "and I'll believe you."

She walked out into weather that had already turned the mud to ice at its edges, thinking not of the creek but of the way he'd said believe — like gold was a story you had to keep telling, one flake at a time, until someone agreed it was true. Six weeks was nothing. She had time.

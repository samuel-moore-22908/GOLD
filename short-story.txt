The Assay

Osei had weighed enough gold to stop being impressed by it, but he still watched people's faces when the number left his mouth. Most flinched. A few went very still, doing arithmetic they didn't want witnesses to see.

The woman in the doorway — Mara, she'd said, though names meant nothing here — hadn't flinched. She'd asked what the number meant, plainly, like she already suspected the answer wouldn't match the weeks it cost her.

He told her: twenty-two carat, a fair weight, worth less than the boots she'd worn through to get here. She didn't argue. She just asked where the good creeks ran, and he found himself sketching the fork on brown paper before he'd decided to answer at all.

That was the trouble with this work. You weighed metal all day and thought you were weighing people too — who'd fold, who wouldn't. Osei had been wrong often enough to know better, and still he kept guessing.

He told her the truth about the upper fork: picked clean two seasons back, not worth the climb. He didn't tell her about the smaller vein past it, the one nobody had filed a claim on, because it barely paid for the trip.

She left before dusk. He watched her go, already recalculating what her silence had been worth, and for once didn't mind being wrong. Some claims, he thought, you don't assay. You just hope.
